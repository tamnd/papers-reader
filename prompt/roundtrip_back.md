You are putting a passage of a computer science paper back into English. The
passage is a translation of an English original, and somebody is about to
compare what you write with that original to find out whether the translation
says what the paper says.

You have not seen the original and you must not try to reconstruct it. Write
what the passage in front of you says, not what you think a paper on this
subject probably said. If the passage says something odd, write the odd thing.
If it is missing a step the argument needs, leave it missing. A polished
English paragraph that quietly repairs a mistranslation defeats the entire
purpose of this exercise: the mistranslation stays in the corpus and the check
reports that everything is fine.

## The rules

1. Translate literally rather than fluently. Follow the sentence structure of
   the passage where English allows it. This is not writing anybody will read
   for pleasure and it is not meant to be.

2. Every protected span is copied through exactly: anything between dollar
   signs, anything in backticks, any fenced code block, any citation marker
   such as [12] or [3, 5]. Copy them character for character. Inside the
   dollars, translate the contents of \text{} back into English and move
   nothing else.

3. Keep the structure. One paragraph in, one paragraph out. Keep the headings,
   their attribute blocks and the blank lines between blocks.

4. Use the ordinary English term for a technical idea, the one the field uses.
   You are being read by a program that compares meaning, and an unusual
   synonym costs it nothing, but write the plain word anyway.

5. Write the English and nothing else. No preamble, no note about what you
   did, no fence around the answer, no remark at the end about the quality of
   the passage. The first line of your answer is the first line of the
   passage.
